Research

How Should a Model Respond to Uncertain Data?

From finitely many measurements, each corrupted by noise, the unknown distribution is only partially characterized. Furthermore, the true distribution generally may not satisfy the assumptions which a method was built on. In my research, I investigate how to design models that are robust to this uncertainty.

  • Data-Driven Dynamical Systems

    Learning how to identify and control systems from noisy data.

  • Distributional Robustness

    Accounting for potential distribution shifts to ensure out-of-sample robustness.

My current preprint on frequency-response estimation explores these themes deeper. Read about it below.

This work is especially applicable to areas where data is scarce and noise can strongly differ from that seen in training, such as:

  • Financial portfolio optimization during black-swan events
  • Training artificial intelligence models that generalize well to unseen data

Current Interests

I am interested in bridging the gap between control theory and artificial intelligence for real-world applications. In particular, I am investigating a distributionally robust framework for denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs). The target application is audio denoising models that generalize well to unseen data.

arXiv preprint 2026 arXiv:2610.09395 [eess.SY] Submitted 7 October 2026

Distributionally Robust Frequency Response Estimation Using Non-Steady-State Frequency-Domain Data

Stefan Damjanovic, John W. Simpson-Porco, and Klaske van Heusden

Abstract ↗ PDF ↗

In Plain Terms

How can we estimate a dynamical system's true steady-state response when measurements contain transients and unusually large disturbances? This work develops a distributionally robust approach to frequency-response estimation from noisy data, including measurements collected before the system reaches steady state.

Results

The theoretical results are tested on a standard fourth-order single-input single-output benchmark system. The tested noise distributions span a broad range of tail heaviness, from Gaussian to Cauchy, and the estimator is compared with an averaged SVD-based baseline.

Error as Tails Become Increasingly Heavy

Mean, median and CVaR₀.₉ relative RMSE (%) of an averaged-SVD baseline and the distributionally robust (DR) estimator across several Student-t noise laws with varying degrees of freedom ν.

  • Averaged SVD baseline
  • Distributionally robust (DR)

The degree of freedom ν of the noise (shown in the horizontal axes above) is explained below in the Explore tab ↓

The mean errors cross near ν ≈ 2, after which the baseline's errors grow quickly while the DR estimator's stay roughly flat: under Cauchy noise the mean is 297.26% for averaged-SVD versus 40.25% for DR, and CVaR₀.₉ is 2,028.46% versus 72.43%.
